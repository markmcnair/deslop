# Structural Pattern Bans

Every structure below is banned. When found, apply the specified fix.

---

## 1. Binary contrasts

**Pattern:** "Not X. Y." / "It's not X, it's Y." / "The answer isn't X. It's Y."

**All banned forms:**
- "Not because X. Because Y."
- "Not because X, but because Y."
- "[X] isn't the problem. [Y] is."
- "The answer isn't X. It's Y."
- "It feels like X. It's actually Y."
- "The question isn't X. It's Y."
- "Not X. But Y."
- "not X, it's Y" / "isn't X, it's Y"
- "stops being X and starts being Y"
- "doesn't mean X, but actually Y"
- "not just X but also Y"
- "It's not just about X, it's about Y"

**Fix:** State your point directly without negation. "The problem is Y" or "Y matters here."

---

## 2. Negative listings

**Pattern:** Revealing what something isn't before revealing what it is.

**Banned forms:**
- "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z."
- "It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z."

**Fix:** State your conclusion first. "It was Z."

---

## 3. Dramatic fragmentation

**Pattern:** Sentence fragments for artificial emphasis.

**Banned forms:**
- "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing]."
- "X. And Y. And Z."
- "This unlocks something. [Word]."
- "Speed. Quality. Cost."

**Fix:** Use complete sentences. Trust your content to carry weight without typographic tricks.

---

## 4. Rhetorical setups

**Pattern:** Announcing an insight instead of delivering it.

**Banned forms:**
- "What if [reframe]?"
- "Here's what I mean:"
- "Think about it:"
- "And that's okay."
- "Let me explain."
- "Consider this:"

**Fix:** State your point. Let readers reach their own conclusions.

---

## 5. Rule of three

**Pattern:** Forcing ideas into groups of three. LLMs do this compulsively.

**Banned forms:**
- Any list of exactly three parallel items used for rhetorical effect
- "X, Y, and Z" when used as a comprehensive-sounding summary
- Three consecutive sentences with the same structure

**Fix:** Use two items. Or one. Or four. Anything except the AI-default three. If you need three things, vary the structure so they don't read as a rhetorical triple.

---

## 6. Anaphora (repeated sentence starters)

**Pattern:** 3+ consecutive sentences starting with the same word or phrase.

**Example:**
- "Your sales team gets... Your ops manager gets... Your front desk gets..."
- "We build... We connect... We train..."

**Fix:** Vary the openings. Start some sentences with the object, some with a transition, some with a different subject.

---

## 7. Question-then-answer

**Pattern:** Asking a question and immediately answering it.

**Banned forms:**
- "What does this mean? It means..."
- "Why does this matter? Because..."
- "Bad answer? We fix it."
- "So what's the takeaway? The takeaway is..."

**Fix:** Make the statement directly. "This means..." or just state the fact.

---

## 8. Superficial -ing phrases

**Pattern:** Tacking present participle phrases onto sentences for fake depth.

**Banned words in this pattern:**
highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating, fostering, encompassing, showcasing, demonstrating, illustrating, representing, signaling

**Fix:** Restructure as a direct statement. "The temple uses blue and gold" not "The temple uses blue and gold, reflecting the regional palette."

---

## 9. Copula avoidance

**Pattern:** Substituting elaborate constructions for "is" or "are".

**Banned forms:**
- "serves as" -> "is"
- "stands as" -> "is"
- "functions as" -> "is"
- "acts as" -> "is"
- "represents" (when meaning "is") -> "is"
- "marks" (when meaning "is") -> "is"
- "boasts" -> "has"
- "features" (as verb for possession) -> "has"
- "offers" (when meaning "has") -> "has"

**Fix:** Use "is", "are", "has", "have". Simple copulas.

---

## 10. Synonym cycling

**Pattern:** Using a different word for the same thing in consecutive sentences to avoid repetition.

**Example:** "The protagonist... The main character... The central figure... The hero..."

**Fix:** Pick one term and use it. Repetition is fine. Forced synonyms are worse.

---

## 11. False ranges

**Pattern:** "From X to Y" constructions where X and Y aren't on a meaningful scale.

**Example:** "from the singularity of the Big Bang to the grand cosmic web, from the birth and death of stars to the enigmatic dance of dark matter"

**Fix:** Just list the topics. "The book covers the Big Bang, star formation, and dark matter theories."

---

## 12. Challenges and future prospects

**Pattern:** Formulaic "challenges" or "outlook" sections.

**Banned forms:**
- "Despite its [positive], [subject] faces several challenges..."
- "Despite these challenges..."
- "Challenges and Legacy"
- "Future Outlook"
- "[Subject] continues to thrive despite..."

**Fix:** Name the specific challenge and what's being done about it. "Traffic increased after the IT parks opened. The city started a drainage project in 2022."

---

## 13. Significance inflation

**Pattern:** Puffing up importance with sweeping claims.

**Banned forms:**
- "stands/serves as a testament to"
- "marking a pivotal moment in"
- "a vital/significant/crucial/pivotal role"
- "underscores/highlights its importance"
- "reflects broader [trends]"
- "symbolizing its ongoing/enduring"
- "setting the stage for"
- "marking/shaping the [field/era]"
- "represents/marks a shift"
- "key turning point"
- "evolving landscape"
- "indelible mark"

**Fix:** State the specific fact without editorial inflation. "The institute was established in 1989 to collect regional statistics."

---

## 14. Promotional language

**Pattern:** Ad-copy tone in non-advertising text.

**Banned words/phrases:**
boasts a, vibrant, rich (figurative), profound, enhancing its, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking (figurative), renowned, breathtaking, must-visit, stunning, world-class

**Fix:** State specific facts. "The town has a weekly market and an 18th-century church" not "a vibrant town with rich cultural heritage and stunning natural beauty."

---

## 15. Passive voice

**Pattern:** Subject receives the action instead of performing it.

**Banned forms:**
- "X was created"
- "It is believed that"
- "Mistakes were made"
- "The decision was reached"
- "It has been noted"
- "Can be seen"

**Fix:** Name the actor. "We created X." "The team decided." Active voice, human subject.

**Allowed:** Passive voice is fine when the actor is unknown or doesn't matter to the reader. "The package was delivered at 3pm." "The check was mailed Tuesday." "Payroll is processed every Friday." Flag it only when a known actor is hiding ("Mistakes were made") or when three or more passive sentences pile up in one paragraph.

---

## 16. False agency

**Pattern:** Giving inanimate things human actions.

**Banned forms:**
- "a complaint becomes a fix"
- "a bet lives or dies"
- "the decision emerges"
- "the culture shifts"
- "the conversation moves toward"
- "the data tells us"
- "the market rewards"
- "this approach unlocks"

**Fix:** Name the person doing the thing. "Buyers purchase" not "the market rewards." "We discovered" not "the data tells us."

---

## 17. Inline-header vertical lists

**Pattern:** Bullet points where each item starts with a bolded header and colon.

**Example:**
- **User Experience:** The interface has been improved.
- **Performance:** Load times are faster.
- **Security:** We added encryption.

**Fix:** Write it as prose. "The update improves the interface, speeds up load times, and adds encryption."

---

## 18. Emoji decoration

**Pattern:** Emojis used as bullet markers or section decorators.

**Fix:** Remove all emojis. Zero tolerance.

---

## 19. Acknowledgment loops

**Pattern:** Repeatedly acknowledging or validating the reader/user.

**Banned forms:**
- "That's a great point."
- "You raise an important issue."
- "I appreciate you bringing this up."
- "That's an excellent observation."

**Fix:** Respond to the substance, not the fact that someone said something.

---

## 20. "Let's" constructions

**Pattern:** Collaborative framing that nobody asked for.

**Banned forms:**
- "Let's explore..."
- "Let's take a look at..."
- "Let's break this down..."
- "Let's dive in..."

**Fix:** Just do the thing. "The pricing breaks down as..." not "Let's break down the pricing..."

---

## 21. Bulleted recaps of simple things

**Pattern:** A short reply that turns two or three plain facts into a bullet list.

**Example:**
"Here's a quick summary:
- Meeting: Tuesday
- Time: 2pm
- Location: My office"

**Fix:** One sentence. "Tuesday at 2 in my office works."

---

## 22. Bold headers in short emails

**Pattern:** Bold section labels ("**Next steps:**", "**Summary:**", "**Action items:**") in an email or message under about 200 words.

**Fix:** Remove the headers. Write it as a normal message. Headers are for documents.

---

## 23. Same three-part reply

**Pattern:** Every reply follows the same shape: thank or acknowledge, answer in a structured block, offer more help. Across a thread, every message looks like a template.

**Fix:** Answer the question. Skip the acknowledgment and the offer unless they add something. Let the shape follow the content. A yes can be one word.

---

## 24. Recap closing paragraph

**Pattern:** A final paragraph that restates what the email already said. "In summary, we'll meet Tuesday, review the draft, and finalize by Friday."

**Fix:** Cut it. If the email is long enough to need a recap, put the one action item at the top instead.

---

## 25. Formal register in a casual channel

**Pattern:** Full paragraphs, greetings, and sign-offs in a text or Slack reply. "Hi Sarah, Thank you for your message. I will be there at 5pm. Best regards."

**Fix:** Match the channel. A text is "I'll be there at 5." Slack threads rarely need a greeting or a sign-off.

---

## 26. Mirrored subject lines and restated questions

**Pattern:** Opening by restating the other person's question. "Regarding your question about the invoice, ..." or "You asked about the timeline."

**Fix:** Answer it. They know what they asked.
